Developmental profile

What changes as they get older

The risk changes shape three times between a child's first tablet and their last year of high school. Each stage needs a different response.

Device-borne risk

Ages 6–9

At this age the child is rarely the target. The devices, apps and organizations around them are.

  • Fake-currency and giveaway tricks inside Roblox and Minecraft. The UK Safer Internet Centre found money losses "including 8-year-olds"
  • Connected toys, kids' watches and home cameras with weak or absent security
  • Unauthorized in-app purchases and manipulative monetization
  • Algorithmic content exposure on YouTube and short-video feeds
  • Evidence gap: no source found segments contact-crime prevalence for this band specifically

The unguarded middle

Ages 10–12

Old enough to use apps alone and lie about their age. Too young for the teen safety features that start at 13.

  • Thorn found that 1 in 6 children aged 9 to 12 report an online sexual interaction, and that 43% of preteens get around age restrictions
  • FBI's 764 / violent-network victim profile starts at age 10
  • Trust trades, item theft and first Discord exposure
  • Exclusion-based cyberbullying begins to dominate

Peak targeting

Ages 13–15

This age group shows the highest recorded rates in almost every category, and the most serious outcomes happen here.

  • Thorn: 53% of sextortion victims were first targeted at 13–15
  • Peer deepfake-nude incidents in schools; nudify-app victimization
  • Cyberbullying at its measured peak; boys report higher rates than girls
  • AI companion chatbot dependency begins; younger teens trust them more than older teens do
  • Skin gambling onset, earliest reported start age 13

Adult-coded fraud

Ages 16–18

They handle money on their own for the first time, with their own payment apps, a first job and a first marketplace account, but without the experience that comes later.

  • Financial sextortion peaks: IWF found 98% of sextortion victims were boys aged 14–17
  • Fake job offers, fake-check scams, money-mule recruitment
  • Crypto and "investment club" pump schemes
  • Marketplace and resale fraud; BBB's 18–24 band shows the highest median loss of any age group
Detection

Signals that cut across classes

Any one of these on its own means very little. Every source that discusses warning signs says the same thing: look for several at once, not for a single indicator.

Conversational

  • "Let's move this to Discord / Telegram / Snap". The move off the original app, which shows up in both grooming and trade scams
  • Secrecy framing: "keep this between us," "don't tell your parents"
  • Flattery out of proportion to how long they have known each other, such as "you're so mature for your age"
  • Manufactured urgency, countdown timers, "claim before it expires," "verify or be banned"
  • Near-identical scripted phrasing recurring across unrelated conversations (Thorn found this in 38% of sextortion chat logs)
  • "Send first and I'll send back". The same structure whether the currency is Robux or images

Behavioral

  • Sudden secrecy around a device, panic at a notification, or the screen being switched when someone walks in
  • Withdrawal from family and offline friends, or from an activity a specific contact has access to
  • Unexplained gifts, game currency, packages or money
  • Attempts to acquire gift cards, crypto, or peer-to-peer payments
  • Fresh wounds or scars in patterns; long sleeves in warm weather (specifically flagged in FBI 764 advisories)
  • An AI chatbot treated as the primary confidant for serious problems

Technical

  • "New device" logins, password-reset emails, or unrecognized active sessions on game and social accounts
  • Heat, battery drain or unexplained data use consistent with a background infostealer or clicker
  • Newly installed executors, mod loaders, cheat tools, or browser extensions
  • Look-alike domains in browser history, using swapped characters and near-miss spellings of real platform names
  • Carrier notifications about SIM or number changes
  • Requests to screen-share or to send a screenshot of a login page. This steals the login session without ever asking for a password
Response

Where to report, by threat type

Current services, named. Two of them share the name "Take It Down" and are easily mixed up, so both are listed.

NCMEC CyberTipline

The central US clearinghouse for child sexual exploitation reports, grooming, sextortion, CSAM. missingkids.org/cybertipline or 1-800-843-5678.

NCMEC Take It Down

For a minor whose explicit image is circulating. Hashes the image on the child's own device so participating platforms can block it, the image itself never leaves. Can be used anonymously.

FTC TakeItDown.ftc.gov

Separate from NCMEC's tool. Where to report a platform that failed its 48-hour removal obligation under the TAKE IT DOWN Act; FTC enforcement began 19 May 2026.

FBI / IC3

ic3.gov, local field offices, or 1-800-CALL-FBI, for sextortion, violent online networks, mule recruitment, and fraud. The FBI's own framing to victims: you are not alone, and this is not your fault.

Report Remove (UK)

Childline and IWF joint service for under-18s in the UK seeking removal of their own intimate images. 1,894 submissions in 2025, up 66%.

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline

US. The first call to make if a child discloses self-harm or suicidal thinking, including when an online group pushed them towards it.

Honesty about the evidence

What this register cannot tell you

Places where popular claims outrun the data, or where the data simply does not exist yet. If you are building something from this, read these before the confident parts.

Gap
No source found segments online contact-crime prevalence for ages 6–9. The youngest band's entries here are device-, platform- and institution-borne because that is what the evidence covers, not because nothing else happens.
Gap
NCMEC's own totals are not consistent across its publications, "50,000 financial sextortion reports," "80,000+ sextortion reports" and "1.4 million online enticement reports" all appear in 2025–2026 NCMEC materials under different category definitions. Treat the trend direction as reliable, not cross-publication arithmetic.
Gap
No current figure isolates SIM swapping of minors, swatting of minor streamers, or teen losses to finfluencer schemes. Adult or all-ages figures are the best available and are labeled as such in the entries.
Contested
The Momo Challenge is a confirmed hoax. India's CBI found no linked death; the NSPCC, Samaritans and UK Safer Internet Centre declared it a moral panic. It should never appear in a product's threat list.
Contested
Blackout-challenge causation is unresolved. The deaths are real and litigated, but the CDC documented 82 probable choking-game deaths among 6–19-year-olds between 1995 and 2007, long before TikTok. Courts have ruled on Section 230 immunity, not on whether an algorithm caused anything.
Contested
Every major AI-chatbot case is still open. Garcia v. Character Technologies, Raine v. OpenAI and Kentucky's suit against Character.AI are all pending. Nothing is adjudicated; describe them as allegations.
Source quality
Several patterns, skin-trading scam mechanics, the Roblox scam taxonomy, Discord Nitro variants, are documented mainly by security vendors and gaming blogs, not government or peer-reviewed data. Useful for direction, but not solid enough to quote as a statistic.
